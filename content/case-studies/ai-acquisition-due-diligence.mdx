## The short version

Underwriting a warehouse acquisition means reconciling leases, tenant concentration, rent assumptions, operating costs and market comparables, usually by hand and in spreadsheets. We built an engine that does the reading with AI, does every calculation in deterministic code, and links every number back to the page it came from.

## Every acquisition is a reconciliation problem

A data room tells the same story several times: in the leases and their amendments, in the seller's rent roll, in the operating statement and in the offering memo. The versions rarely agree, and the bid depends on finding where they don't.

| Figure | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| 4–8 hours | to abstract one commercial lease by hand (1) |
| 86%+ | of spreadsheets in recent field audits contained errors (2) |
| 1%+ | of formula cells wrong, even after careful development (2) |
| 6.9% | US industrial vacancy at mid-2026, with leasing at a four-year high (3) |

For a portfolio of 39 leases, the published range of 4 to 8 hours per lease implies 156 to 312 analyst hours of abstraction before reconciliation or modelling starts. Industrial and logistics also made up 55% of US net-lease investment in Q4 2025 (4), so these teams see a steady flow of deals that all need the same checks.

## Architecture

Language models do what they're good at: reading messy documents into a strict schema. Everything after that is ordinary, testable code.

**The data room** holds leases, amendments, the rent roll (XLSX), T-12 statements, property details, and market rents and sales. It goes through three steps:

1. **Ingest and classify.** Document typing, de-duplication, layout-aware parsing, OCR for scans, and a page and bounding-box index.
2. **LLM structured extraction.** Schema-constrained JSON per clause: value, verbatim snippet and page, with amendments applied in date order.
3. **Validate.** Type, date and arithmetic rules; the rent schedule must reproduce the lease; low confidence goes to a review queue.

Then the **deterministic core** takes over. It's Python, with no language model in the maths:

| Module | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Reconciliation | Lease vs rent roll vs T-12, tolerances per field, overlaps, holdovers, missing documents |
| Financial engine | Lease-by-lease monthly cash flow; NOI, WALE, occupancy, DSCR; cap rate, DCF, IRR, NPV; rollover, TI and commissions |
| Scenario engine | Vacancy and downtime, market rent shocks, exit-cap expansion, refinancing, break options |
| Geospatial comps | PostGIS distance filter, recency and size weights, implied cap rate and $/sf, feeding valuation and exit |

The outputs are an **investment dashboard** (React, with deal comparison), a **risk register** where every item cites its page, an **underwriting report** generated as a PDF per deal, and an **editable model** with assumptions that exports to Excel.

- **API and jobs:** FastAPI, background workers for parsing and extraction, PostgreSQL with a JSONB evidence store and full audit log.
- **Security:** per-deal access control, encryption at rest, model providers configured not to retain or train on client documents.
- **Quality:** data-quality, reconciliation and reference-model tests run on every change.

## Layer 1: every field comes with its evidence

The extractor returns a value only if it can quote where the value came from. A number with no snippet and page reference is treated as missing, not guessed.

| Field | What is captured | Validation before it's used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Term | Commencement, expiry, amendments in date order | Start before expiry; latest amendment wins |
| Base rent | Rent per sf or annual amount, rent table | Schedule must reproduce the lease's own rent table |
| Escalations | Fixed bumps, CPI reviews with floor and cap | Collar applied; review dates fall on anniversaries |
| Free rent | Abated months and what stays payable | Abatement inside the term |
| Renewal options | Number, length, rent basis | Option notice dates inside the term |
| Break clauses | Effective date, notice, penalty | Flagged to the risk register if inside the hold |
| Recoveries | NNN, modified gross with base year, gross | Structure checked against T-12 recoveries |
| Deposit, area | Security deposit, rentable square feet | Compared with rent roll within tolerance |

One extracted field, as stored:

```json
{
  "field": "expiry_date",
  "value": "2029-06-30",
  "source": { "doc": "Lease_B-12_Amendment-2.pdf", "page": 1 },
  "snippet": "the Expiration Date is amended to 30 June 2029"
}
```

From the evaluation data room. The amendment shortened the term by two years; the seller's rent roll still showed the original date (rent roll expiry 30 Jun 2031 vs lease 30 Jun 2029).

### How extraction quality is measured

- Hand-abstracted gold leases, scored field by field for precision and recall.
- Exact match for dates and amounts; normalised match for clause text.
- Low-confidence or conflicting fields go to an analyst queue, never straight into the model.
- The benchmark on this page starts from lease abstracts with page references (330 in total), so it tests everything after extraction.

## Layer 2: three sources, checked against each other

Each rent-roll row is matched to its lease and compared field by field. Each asset's T-12 is compared with the rent and recoveries the leases actually support. Anything outside tolerance becomes a flag with both documents attached.

| Check | Tolerance |
| --- | --- |
| Monthly rent | 0.5% or $50, whichever is larger |
| Rentable area | 0.5% |
| Start, expiry, next step | Exact date |
| Recovery structure, deposit | Exact |
| Free rent, holdover | Status vs lease term |
| Overlaps, missing leases | Any occurrence |
| T-12 base rent | 2.5% of lease-supported rent |
| T-12 recoveries | 5% of lease-supported recoveries |

### The benchmark

We planted 16 realistic errors of 13 kinds into the evaluation data room, plus rounding and remeasurement noise that should *not* be flagged, and kept the answer key separate from the engine. The engine caught **16 of 16** errors with **0** false flags from the noise, across **411** field and statement checks, of which **96.1%** agreed automatically.

## Where the asking price holds, and where it doesn't

At the asking price of $549.5m, the portfolio returns 6.3% unlevered against a 7% target. The engine solves the price that clears the target: $522.6m, or $26.9m below the ask, and shows which asset carries the gap.

| Asset | Ask | Comps value | Going-in cap | Comps cap | 10-yr IRR | Price at 7% |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Asset A, bulk distribution centre | $276.5m | $272.1m | 5.02% | 5.10% | 4.8% | $234.1m |
| Asset B, multi-tenant business park | $124.5m | $110.1m | 5.38% | 6.09% | 6.8% | $122.5m |
| Asset C, last-mile infill portfolio | $148.5m | $153.0m | 4.79% | 4.65% | 8.4% | $166.0m |
| Portfolio | $549.5m | $535.2m | 5.04% | | 6.3% | $522.6m |

**Reading it:** Asset C clears the target with room to spare, because in-place rents sit 14% below market. Asset B is priced 13% above what its comparables support. Asset A sits $42.4m above its price at target, more than the whole portfolio gap, which Asset C's $17.5m of headroom partly offsets. Its largest tenant pays 61% of the rent, the lease rolls in 2031, and the ask implies a cap rate below the comparables.

Comparable sales are weighted by distance, recency and size: comps more than 25 miles from the subject, or older than 24 months, are excluded. The weighted comps cap is 5.10% for Asset A, 6.09% for Asset B and 4.65% for Asset C. Exit cap = weighted comps cap + 25 bps. Comps value = year-1 NOI ÷ weighted comps cap.

## Stress-testing the bid

Each scenario re-runs the full lease-by-lease model, not a shortcut on the totals. All 11 ran in 0.2 seconds on a laptop. Rent and exit cap move returns most.

| --- | --- |
| Exit cap +100 bps | −134 bps |
| Market rent −10% | −117 bps |
